# 国际贸易诈骗的10种常见套路（货代诈骗/钓鱼邮件）

做外贸最怕的不是客户难缠，而是钱款在不经意间被骗子截走。定金刚打出去，真正的供应商却来电说"我们没收到钱"；货发到一半，货代突然失联，货物与提单一起消失。这类国际贸易诈骗往往伪装成正常交易，等你发现时，资金可能已被层层转移、洗白。受骗后每拖延一个月，追回难度就增加一分、证据流失一分、跨境执行成本也涨一分。识别套路、提前设防，是成本最低的自保方式——即便真被骗，第一时间寻求国际法律建议也不迟，但代价要大得多。

## 十种最常见的套路

1. **钓鱼邮件（BEC/邮箱入侵）**：骗子黑进供应商或你的邮箱，冒充对方发来"变更收款账户"邮件。凡变更账户，务必电话或视频二次核验，并保留邮件原文含邮件头，供后续国际法律追责取证。
2. **货代诈骗**：冒用知名货代名义，或注册一个名称只差一两个字母的"李鬼"公司，收取运费、订舱费、保证金后卷款失联，货物被扣在异地甚至直接丢失。
3. **假客户"定金陷阱"**：伪造采购身份的骗子开出诱人订单，要求你先付"佣金""保证金"，收款后人间蒸发。
4. **伪造单证（提单/质检报告）**：用假提单、假质检报告骗取货款，或掩盖货不对板、短装的事实。
5. **预付款/样品费连环收费**：以"样品费、检测费、认证费"名义小额多次收费，单笔不大却积少成多，且始终没有真实订单。
6. **信用证不符点拒付**：故意利用信用证条款的细微不符点拒付，货已发出却拿不到钱。
7. **"国家采购/大单"骗局**：冒充政府或大型机构采购，要求缴纳"注册费、中标费、保证金"。
8. **中间商卷款**：中间商收到货款后不向下游转付，携款跑路。
9. **货到付款争议**：收货后以质量问题为由拒付，或伪造签收记录、质检争议来赖账。
10. **跨境投资/代理骗局**：以高回报合作、独家代理为饵，骗取前期投入后销声匿迹。

## 通用识别红线

- 收款账户名称与发票抬头、公司所在地不符，且频繁变动；
- 官方域名或邮箱与正牌公司只差一个字母（如 `.com` 变 `.cm`），或使用免费邮箱后缀；
- 突然催促付款、施加时间压力，拒绝电话、视频核验；
- 承诺"稳赚不赔"的高回报，或对第三方尽调、背景询问避而不谈。

## 被骗后的应对步骤

1. **立即止损**：停止一切后续付款，通知银行止付、冻结相关账户；
2. **固定证据**：完整保存邮件头、聊天记录、合同、发票、汇款水单，必要时做公证保全；
3. **报案**：携带证据向公安报案，争取对涉案账户紧急止付、冻结；
4. **法律追收**：委托律师评估刑事报案与民事/仲裁追偿并行路径，以及境外判决的承认与执行；
5. **排查风险**：检查邮箱转发规则与后台权限，防范二次诈骗与连锁损失。

## 风险提示

- 勿信"黑客追款""内部关系追回"等第三方服务——这是二次诈骗的高发区；
- 勿删除或"整理"原始证据，原始数据比整理过的更可信；
- 变更收款账户、要求预付第三方费用、域名仅差一个字母，是三大高频危险信号；
- 涉及境外法律适用与判决执行时，请以当地法律或专业意见为准。

# 10 Common International Trade Scams and How to Spot Them

The worst part of cross-border trade is rarely a difficult customer — it is money silently diverted by fraudsters. You send the deposit, then the real supplier calls: "We never received the money." Or your shipment is halfway to port when the freight forwarder goes dark, taking both cargo and bill of lading with them. International trade scams are designed to look like normal transactions, and by the time you realize, the funds have often been layered and moved. Every month of delay raises recovery difficulty, lets critical evidence slip, and drives up cross-border enforcement costs. Learning to recognize these scams up front is your cheapest protection — and if you are already a victim, seeking international legal advice early still beats waiting, but at a far higher price.

## The ten most common scams

1. **Phishing / business email compromise (BEC)**: Hackers break into the supplier's or your mailbox and send a forged "changed bank account" email. Whenever an account changes, verify by phone or video call — and preserve the full email with headers to support any later international legal action.
2. **Freight forwarder fraud**: Scammers impersonate a reputable forwarder, or register a lookalike company one letter off from the real name, collect freight, booking, or deposit fees, then vanish — leaving your cargo seized abroad or simply lost.
3. **Fake buyer "deposit trap"**: A scammer posing as a buyer offers an irresistible order and demands an upfront "commission" or "guarantee deposit," then disappears once paid.
4. **Forged documents (bill of lading / inspection reports)**: Fake bills of lading or inspection reports are used to extract payment or conceal short-shipped or substandard goods.
5. **Small-fee drip charges**: Repeated small demands for "sample fees, testing fees, certification fees" that add up, with no real order behind them.
6. **Letter-of-credit discrepancies**: Deliberately exploiting minor discrepancies to refuse payment after the goods have shipped.
7. **"Government procurement / big order" scam**: Impersonating governments or large institutions and demanding "registration, bid, or guarantee fees."
8. **Runaway middleman**: An agent or intermediary collects payment but never forwards it downstream, then flees.
9. **Post-delivery payment disputes**: The buyer takes goods, then refuses to pay citing quality, or fabricates signature and inspection disputes.
10. **Fake investment / agency schemes**: High-return partnerships or exclusive agency offers that bait upfront capital, then vanish.

## Universal red flags

- The receiving account does not match the invoice name or the company's country, and it keeps changing;
- The domain or email differs from the genuine firm by a single letter (e.g. `.com` vs `.cm`), or uses a free-mail domain;
- Sudden pressure to pay fast, with refusal to do a phone or video check;
- "Guaranteed" high returns, or evasion of due-diligence questions.

## What to do if you have been scammed

1. **Stop the bleeding**: Halt all further payments; ask your bank to stop, reverse, or freeze the relevant accounts;
2. **Secure evidence**: Preserve full email headers, chat logs, contracts, invoices, and transfer receipts; notarize where possible;
3. **Report**: File a complaint with law enforcement and seek emergency freeze of the receiving accounts;
4. **Recover through counsel**: Assess criminal reporting in parallel with civil or arbitration claims, and cross-border enforcement of any judgment;
5. **Audit**: Check mailbox forwarding rules and account permissions to prevent a second attack.

## Key risks

- Do not trust "hacker recovery" or "inside-connection recovery" services — this is a common second scam;
- Do not delete or "tidy" original evidence; raw data is more credible than cleaned data;
- Changed bank accounts, requests to prepay third-party fees, and one-letter-off domains are three high-frequency danger signals;
- Where foreign law or enforcement applies, rely on local law or professional advice.
