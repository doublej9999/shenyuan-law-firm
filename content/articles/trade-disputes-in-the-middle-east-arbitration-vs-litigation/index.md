# 中东贸易纠纷：仲裁还是诉讼？（迪拜/沙特）

你给迪拜或沙特的客户发了货，对方以"标准不符""沙里亚合规异议"等理由拒付款，还反过来要你赔偿。中东是重要买家市场，但当地法律体系融合了成文法与伊斯兰法（沙里亚）原则，诉讼程序、证据要求和对利息的态度都可能与中国习惯完全不同。走错路径，除了多花几个月，还可能因程序或证据形式问题被驳回。更现实的是，即便你赢了，能否在当地执行还取决于一系列特殊规则。下面把迪拜与沙特两条主流路径的差别讲清楚，让你在纠纷之初就选对打法。

## 第一步：先看合同约定，别做无用功

- **仲裁条款优先**：多数中东商事合同约定了仲裁（如 DIFC-LCIA、DIAC、SCCAR 或沙特商业仲裁中心），有约定就老实按仲裁走；
- **没有约定再看法院体系**：迪拜有普通法院与 DIFC 自贸区法院双轨，沙特近年推行专门商事法院，管辖规则需仔细甄别；
- **留好全部书面往来**：报价单、订单、发票、验货报告、WhatsApp/邮件记录都要保存备证。

## 第二步：仲裁与诉讼怎么选

- **仲裁（Arbitration）**：迪拜 DIFC-LCIA 等自贸区仲裁、沙特商事仲裁中心逐步与国际接轨，程序灵活、保密、一裁终局，裁决相对易在《纽约公约》框架下跨境执行，是跨境商务的主流选择；缺点同样是费用较高、需指定仲裁员与地点；
- **法院诉讼（Litigation）**：当地法院对本国判决执行力强，但对外国当事人而言，程序陌生、语言与翻译要求严、周期较长；部分裁决/判决在沙特还需遵循其对利息等事项的特殊规定；
- **考虑自贸区法院**：迪拜的 DIFC 法院采用普通法程序、语言为英语，对习惯了普通法流程的中外资方更友好，执行也较顺畅，是介于仲裁与普通法院之间的可行选项。

## 第三步：执行阶段看门道

- 拿到裁决/判决后，真正的考验在**在当地法院登记与执行**；仲裁裁决在迪拜/沙特多大程度被承认，取决于其国内法及与《纽约公约》的关系，务必依据具体情况核实；
- 执行阶段配合当地律师调查客户账户、存货、不动产等可执行资产；
- 留意当地对外国文件的公证、翻译与认证（legalization/apostille）要求，缺一道手续都可能卡住执行。

## 时间与费用预期

| 环节 | 经验周期 | 主要成本 |
|------|---------|---------|
| 证据梳理与协商 | 数天至数周 | 低-中 |
| 仲裁/诉讼至裁决 | 6–18 个月，视法域 | 律师费+程序费 |
| 当地登记与执行 | 6–18 个月 | 执行地律师费+法院费用 |

以上均为经验区间，具体取决于货值、争议复杂度、对方所在地法与程序选择——**我们不承诺结果**，但尽早固定证据、选对程序往往决定能否追回货款。

## 风险提示

- 切忌无视合同已约定的仲裁条款，擅自进军普通法院可能白费功夫；
- 不要忽视公证、翻译与认证程序，否则证据或文书可能不被当地接受；
- 不要被"保证回款"话术误导，中东执行变量多，请对方书面说明路径与收费；
- 中国法律与迪拜/沙特法律差异巨大，拿不准的以当地法律/专业意见为准。

# Disputes in the Middle East: Arbitration vs Litigation

You shipped goods to a buyer in Dubai or Saudi Arabia, and the client refuses to pay — citing "non-conforming standards" or "Sharia compliance objections" — and asks you to compensate them instead. The Middle East is a major buyer market, but its legal systems blend civil law with Islamic (Sharia) principles, and procedure, evidence requirements, and attitudes toward interest can all differ sharply from Chinese practice. The wrong path means months lost or a claim dismissed on procedural or evidentiary grounds. Even if you win, enforcement depends on a set of special local rules. Here is a clear comparison of the two main routes across Dubai and Saudi Arabia so you choose well from the start.

## Step 1: follow the contract first, avoid wasted effort

- **Honor the arbitration clause**: most Gulf commercial contracts name an arbitral forum (DIFC-LCIA, DIAC, SCCAR, or the Saudi Commercial Arbitration Center); if one is agreed, use it;
- **If there is no clause, examine the court landscape**: Dubai has both ordinary courts and the DIFC free-zone courts, while Saudi Arabia has introduced specialized commercial courts — jurisdiction rules need careful review;
- **Keep all written records**: quotations, POs, invoices, inspection reports, and WhatsApp/email threads for evidence.

## Step 2: choose between arbitration and litigation

- **Arbitration**: DIFC-LCIA and other free-zone seats, and the Saudi Commercial Arbitration Center, are increasingly aligned with international practice — flexible, confidential, final, and awards travel relatively well under the New York Convention, making it mainstream for cross-border commerce; the trade-off is higher cost and the need to appoint counsel and a venue;
- **Court litigation**: local courts enforce their own judgments strongly, but for foreign parties the procedure feels unfamiliar, with strict language and translation demands and long timelines; some awards/judgments in Saudi Arabia also face special rules on matters such as interest;
- **Consider the DIFC courts**: Dubai's DIFC courts use common-law procedure and English and are friendlier to parties used to international practice, with relatively smooth enforcement — a viable middle option between arbitration and the ordinary courts.

## Step 3: watch enforcement closely

- Once you have an award or judgment, the real test is **registration and enforcement with the local courts**; how far awards are recognized in Dubai/Saudi depends on their domestic law and relationship to the New York Convention — verify on the specifics;
- During enforcement, work with local counsel to trace the buyer's bank accounts, stock, and property;
- Note local requirements for notarization, translation, and authentication (legalization/apostille) of foreign documents — a missing step can stall enforcement.

## Time and cost expectations

| Stage | Typical timeline | Main costs |
|-------|------------------|------------|
| Evidence review and negotiation | Days to weeks | Low–medium |
| Arbitration/litigation to award | 6–18 months, by jurisdiction | Counsel + procedural fees |
| Local registration and enforcement | 6–18 months | Enforcement counsel + court costs |

These are experience ranges; actual figures depend on value, complexity, counterparty location, and forum — **we do not promise results**, but securing evidence early and choosing the right procedure often determines whether the trade debt is recovered.

## Key risks

- Do not ignore the arbitration clause already in the contract and venture into ordinary courts by mistake;
- Do not overlook notarization, translation, and authentication, or documents may be rejected locally;
- Do not be misled by "guaranteed recovery" promises — Gulf enforcement has many variables; ask for the path, credentials, and fees in writing;
- China law differs greatly from Dubai/Saudi law; when in doubt, follow local law and professional advice.
