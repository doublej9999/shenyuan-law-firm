# 佣金纠纷：海外中介与业务员讨要佣金怎么办

海外中介帮你谈成了客户、订单也顺利出货回款，可到了结算佣金时，对方却说"这单不算我谈的""客户是我带来的"。你仔细一想，当初口头说好、微信上提了一句，合同里根本没写清佣金比例和计算基数。拖着不付，中介可能转向在国外起诉你、甚至到处散播负面信息；贸然多付，又怕对方狮子大开口。下面把佣金纠纷怎么处理讲清楚。

## 第一步：先盘点有哪些证据

- 把双方往来记录整理出来：合同/协议、邮件、聊天记录、报价单、订单、付款凭证、佣金计算表；
- 重点固定三件事：**是否约定了佣金**、**比例或金额**、**计算基数与结算时点**；
- 若只有口头约定，可尝试用邮件/书面方式向对方"确认"关键事实（如"按之前谈好的 3%"），让对方默认或回应，从而固定证据——留意不要虚构内容。

## 第二步：看合同是否约定了明确的佣金条款

- 佣金纠纷九成出在"没说清"：没有书面协议、或只有一句"按行业惯例"，结算时自然各执一词；
- 若合同写明了比例、基数（按合同额还是到账额）、何时付、谁负责付（买方付款后才付等），按约定结算即可；
- 若没有约定或约定不明，可参考交易习惯、双方过往结算记录与当地法律判断——各国对"居间报酬"的规定不同，拿不准的以当地法律/专业意见为准。

## 第三步：先协商，给双方一个对账机会

- 主动发起对账：把本金、到账情况、佣金计算过程列成书面清单发给对方，请其确认；
- 协商中争取"分期支付"或"以双方认可的数据为准"，避免为小数字打持久战；
- 若对方主张的范围明显超乎常理（例如把无关订单也算进来），要求其提供对应订单凭证。

## 第四步：协商不成再考虑正式途径

- 国际贸易佣金纠纷若无法通过协商解决，涉及金额不大时可先发出**律师函**表明态度，不少纠纷会在此阶段解决；
- 若涉及跨境，要判断在**哪国/哪个法域**处理：看合同约定的管辖与适用法律，没有约定则看合同签订地、履行地等连接点；
- 金额大或对方已提起诉讼时，再考虑仲裁或诉讼。跨境诉讼周期长、成本高，务必先评估"赢了能否执行"再决定。

## 时间与费用预期

| 路径 | 经验周期 | 主要成本 |
|------|---------|---------|
| 对账与协商 | 数天至1个月 | 低 |
| 律师函 | 数天至2周 | 固定函件费 |
| 调解/仲裁 | 2–8个月不等 | 仲裁/调解费+律师费 |
| 诉讼及执行 | 6–18个月，视法域 | 诉讼费+律师费+跨境执行费 |

以上均为经验区间，实际取决于金额大小、对方态度与所在法域。我们不承诺结果，但证据是否完整、管辖是否选对，往往决定最终能否顺利拿到佣金。

## 风险提示

- 不要只凭口头承诺就开展合作，务必把佣金条款写进书面合同；
- 不要为了省钱跳过仲裁/管辖条款——这是跨境纠纷中最省成本的约定；
- 不要故意少算基数或拖延对账，否则对方可能在国外起诉，成本与风险反而更高；
- 涉及外币结算与不同国家法律，拿不准的以当地法律/专业意见为准，切勿凭"行业惯例"想当然。

# Commission Disputes with Overseas Sales Agents

An overseas agent helped you land the buyer, the order shipped, and payment came in. But at settlement time the agent argues, "This deal was not negotiated by me" or "The client was mine to begin with." Now you recall it was settled over a chat—there was never a written commission rate or basis. If you hold back payment, the agent may sue you abroad or bad-mouth you; if you pay too much, you worry it is a shakedown. Here is how to handle a commission dispute properly.

## Step 1: take stock of the evidence

- Assemble all correspondence: contracts or agreements, emails, chat records, quotations, orders, payment vouchers, and commission calculations;
- Focus on three facts: **whether commission was agreed**, **the rate or amount**, and **the base and timing of settlement**;
- If the agreement was only oral, try to confirm the key facts in writing by email (e.g., "per the 3% we discussed"), letting the other side respond or acquiesce to fix the evidence—without fabricating content.

## Step 2: check whether the contract actually defines commission

- Nine disputes in ten come from an unclear deal: no written agreement, or only a line about "industry practice," leaving each side reading it differently;
- If the contract states the rate, the base (contract value vs. amount actually received), when to pay, and who pays, settle according to the terms;
- If it is silent or ambiguous, look to trade usage, prior settlement records, and local law—rules on intermediary remuneration differ by country, so follow local law/professional advice where unclear.

## Step 3: negotiate and open a reconciliation

- Take the initiative: turn principal, receipts, and the commission calculation into a written statement and ask the agent to confirm;
- During negotiation, consider installment payment or agreeing to use a jointly recognized dataset, to avoid a long war over a small figure;
- If the claim clearly overreaches (for example, dragging in unrelated orders), ask them to produce the corresponding order documents.

## Step 4: escalate only if negotiation fails

- For smaller amounts, a **demand letter** often resolves the matter at that stage;
- Cross-border matters involve deciding **where and under which law** to proceed: check any jurisdiction and governing-law clause, and absent one, look to where the contract was made, where it was performed, and similar connecting factors;
- For large amounts, or once the other side has sued, consider arbitration or litigation. Cross-border suits are slow and costly—first assess whether a winning judgment can actually be enforced.

## Time and cost expectations

| Route | Typical timeline | Main costs |
|-------|------------------|------------|
| Reconciliation and negotiation | Days to 1 month | Low |
| Demand letter | Days to 2 weeks | Fixed letter fee |
| Mediation / arbitration | 2–8 months, varies | Arbitration/mediation + counsel |
| Litigation and enforcement | 6–18 months, jurisdiction-dependent | Fees + counsel + cross-border enforcement |

These are experience ranges; actual figures depend on amount, the other side's attitude, and the jurisdiction. We do not promise results, but complete evidence and a well-chosen forum often decide whether you ultimately get paid.

## Key risks

- Do not rely on a handshake—put commission terms in a written contract;
- Do not skip the arbitration/jurisdiction clause to save a little; it is the cheapest protection in a cross-border dispute;
- Do not under-count the base or stall reconciliation, or the other side may sue abroad at higher cost and risk to you;
- With foreign-currency and multi-country issues, follow local law/professional advice where unclear rather than assuming "industry practice."
