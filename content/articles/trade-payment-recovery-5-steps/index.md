# 海外客户拖欠货款怎么办？律师教你5步合法追收

货发出去了，尾款迟迟不到；催了几次，客户先是找借口，后来干脆不回复。这是外贸企业最常见的场景，也是最容易"拖成死账"的场景——很多企业等到客户失联才想起找律师，此时证据、时效和资产都可能已经受损。

欠款追收是有"窗口期"的。越早行动，手段越多、成本越低、回款概率越高。以下5步是我们在实务中反复验证的追收路径，供遇到同类问题的企业参考。

## 第1步：固定证据，评估时效（1–3天）

先别急着翻脸，先把"底牌"整理清楚：

- 合同、订单、形式发票、装箱单、提单、报关单；
- 付款记录（银行水单、收款回执）、对账单；
- 与客户沟通的邮件、微信、WhatsApp记录，特别是承认欠款、承诺付款的内容；
- 客户公司全称、注册地址、联系人、股东信息。

同时确认一个关键日期：**诉讼时效**。多数法域对商事债权的时效在2–6年之间，从"知道或应当知道权利受损"起算；如果客户曾书面确认欠款或部分还款，时效可能重新起算。判断标准请以合同约定和适用法律为准，必要时尽快咨询律师。

## 第2步：发出正式催款函（1–2周）

催款函不只是"再催一次"，它的法律意义在于：留下书面催讨记录、中断/重算时效、给客户一个体面的台阶。实务中，一封措辞专业的律师函能解决相当比例的拖欠——尤其是客户本身只是资金紧张、并不想对簿公堂的情况。

催款函应写明：欠款金额与到期日、合同依据、宽限期、逾期将采取的措施（利息、仲裁/诉讼、财产保全、信用记录影响），并限期回复。

## 第3步：协商还款方案（2–6周）

如果客户有还款意愿但资金紧张，可以协商分期还款、延期、部分减免或以货抵债。协商方案务必书面化，并由对方有权签字人签署，最好增加担保（母公司担保、个人担保、抵押）。

**注意**：口头承诺没有证据价值。任何让步都要以"对方先履约或同步提供担保"为前提，避免"再给他一个月"无限循环。

## 第4步：仲裁或诉讼（3–12个月，取决于法域）

协商失败后，需要依据合同约定的争议解决条款选择路径：

- 约定仲裁：按仲裁机构规则推进，裁决可在《纽约公约》160多个缔约国执行；
- 约定诉讼：在合同管辖法院起诉，判决依各国互惠/条约执行；
- 无约定：根据合同履行地、被告所在地等确定管辖。

同时可申请**财产保全**（冻结账户、查封货物/房产），防止客户转移资产。保全动作通常需要在证据充分后尽快进行——这也是"越早行动越有利"的核心原因之一。

## 第5步：执行（6–24个月，视资产情况）

拿到判决或裁决只是第一步。执行的关键在于**找到可执行资产**：银行账户、房产、股权、应收账款、在途货物。跨境执行通常需要当地律师协助，各国流程差异很大。

## 时间与费用预期

| 阶段 | 通常周期 | 主要成本 |
|------|---------|---------|
| 催款函 | 1–2周 | 固定费用，较低 |
| 协商 | 2–6周 | 律师时费或固定费 |
| 仲裁/诉讼 | 3–12个月 | 律师费+仲裁费/诉讼费+保全费 |
| 执行 | 6–24个月 | 律师费+调查费+执行费用 |

以上为经验区间，实际取决于案情复杂度和所在法域。追收类案件部分可按成功收费方式洽谈，具体以评估为准。

## 风险提示

- 不要因客户"承诺付款"就无限期等待——拖过时效将丧失胜诉权；
- 不要自行采用威胁、骚扰等不当手段——可能构成违法并反噬证据效力；
- 不要等到客户破产清算才行动——破产程序中普通债权受偿顺序靠后。

# What to Do When an Overseas Buyer Won't Pay: A 5-Step Legal Guide

The goods shipped. The final payment never came. After a few reminders, the buyer made excuses — then stopped replying altogether. This is the most common scenario in export trade, and the easiest one to let slide into a dead debt. Many businesses only call a lawyer once the buyer has vanished, by which time evidence, limitation periods, and assets may already be compromised.

Debt recovery has a window. The earlier you act, the more tools you have, the lower the cost, and the higher the chance of recovery. These are the five steps we use in practice:

## Step 1: Secure evidence and check the limitation period (1–3 days)

Before anything else, get your paperwork in order:

- Contract, purchase order, pro forma invoice, packing list, bill of lading, customs documents;
- Payment records (bank statements, receipts) and account statements;
- Emails, WeChat, and WhatsApp exchanges — especially anything where the buyer acknowledges the debt or promises payment;
- The buyer's full legal name, registered address, contacts, and shareholder information.

Also confirm one critical date: the **statute of limitations**. In most jurisdictions, commercial claims must be pursued within roughly 2–6 years from when the claim accrued (or when the creditor knew of it). A written acknowledgment of the debt or a partial payment may restart the clock. Confirm the applicable rules with counsel early.

## Step 2: Issue a formal demand letter (1–2 weeks)

A demand letter is not just another reminder. It creates a written record, can interrupt or restart the limitation period, and gives the buyer a dignified way to pay. In practice, a professionally drafted lawyer's letter resolves a meaningful share of overdue invoices — especially where the buyer is merely cash-strapped and does not want litigation.

The letter should state: the amount and due date, the contractual basis, a grace period, and the consequences of non-payment (interest, arbitration or litigation, asset preservation, credit impact), with a deadline to respond.

## Step 3: Negotiate a written repayment plan (2–6 weeks)

If the buyer has intent but no liquidity, consider instalments, an extension, a partial discount, or payment in goods. Whatever you agree must be documented in writing and signed by a person with authority — ideally with added security (parent guarantee, personal guarantee, or collateral).

Remember: verbal promises have no evidentiary value. Any concession should be conditional on the buyer performing first or providing security — otherwise "one more month" repeats forever.

## Step 4: Arbitration or litigation (3–12 months, depending on jurisdiction)

If negotiation fails, follow the dispute resolution clause in your contract:

- Arbitration: proceed under the agreed institution; awards are enforceable in 160+ New York Convention states;
- Litigation: sue in the contractually agreed court; judgments are enforced via reciprocity or treaty;
- No clause: jurisdiction is determined by performance place, defendant location, and similar factors.

Consider **asset preservation** (freezing accounts, seizing goods or property) to stop the buyer moving assets. Preservation should be filed as early as the evidence supports it — another reason early action pays.

## Step 5: Enforcement (6–24 months, asset-dependent)

A judgment or award is only the beginning. Enforcement depends on locating executable assets: bank accounts, real estate, equity, receivables, goods in transit. Cross-border enforcement generally requires local counsel, and procedures differ sharply by jurisdiction.

## Time and cost expectations

| Stage | Typical duration | Main costs |
|-------|------------------|------------|
| Demand letter | 1–2 weeks | Fixed fee, low |
| Negotiation | 2–6 weeks | Hourly or fixed fee |
| Arbitration / litigation | 3–12 months | Counsel fees + tribunal/court fees + preservation |
| Enforcement | 6–24 months | Counsel fees + investigation + execution costs |

These are experience ranges; actual figures depend on complexity and jurisdiction. For recovery matters, success-fee arrangements may be available — subject to assessment and applicable rules.

## Key risks

- Do not wait indefinitely because the buyer "promised to pay" — the limitation period may expire;
- Do not use threats or harassment — it may be unlawful and can undermine your evidence;
- Do not wait until the buyer enters insolvency — unsecured claims rank low in liquidation.
