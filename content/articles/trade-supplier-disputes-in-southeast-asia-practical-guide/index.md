# 东南亚（越南/印尼/泰国）供应商纠纷处理实务

你下单给越南、印尼或泰国的供应商，以为价格便宜产能稳定，结果货期一拖再拖、到货质量不合格，甚至对方关机失联。东南亚供应链成本优势明显，但跨境距离远、语言与法律体系不同，纠纷一旦发生，你飞过去打官司的成本往往比货值还高。更麻烦的是，各国法律制度差异很大——越南偏成文法、印尼有自己独特的商事规则、泰国对证据形式要求严格，用一套中国经验去硬套，多半会碰壁。每当纠纷拖延，对方就有机会把货转卖、把公司注销、让索赔无从下手。下面给出一条按东南亚各国实际操作的完整路径。

## 第一步：先落纸面证据，再谈任何交涉

- **把合同和往来记录归档**：采购订单（PO）、发票、装箱单、质检报告、WhatsApp/邮件往来、付款凭证全部保存，明确交货期、质量标准、验收方式与争议解决条款；
- **发书面催告函（Notice of Breach）**：用可签收方式（邮件+快递）向供应商发出违约通知，写明违约事实、补救期限与拟索赔金额，这在当地法院属于关键程序要件；
- **确认适用法律与争议解决方式**：很多东南亚合同没有写明约定，出事后才靠当地法兜底，务必先弄清楚合同总体约定，以及是否需要公证翻译。

## 第二步：按各国特点选择处理路径

- **越南**：商事仲裁较为常用（如 VIAC），合同约定了仲裁就按约定走；其民事诉讼法对书面证据要求较高，催告函与往来文件务必保留原件；
- **印尼**：商事纠纷多通过法院诉讼或仲裁裁决执行，印尼法律对外国文件的公证、翻译与认证（legalization）要求严格，主张前要先把证据"本地化"；
- **泰国**：对证据形式与时效很敏感，催告应是正式书面文件；小额纠纷也可考虑调解或替代性争议解决（ADR），成本更低。
- **谈判与和解**：多数供应商纠纷仍以协商收场，可谈分期补货、降价赔偿或终止合作换取部分退款，务必把每项安排写成双方签署的书面文件。

## 第三步：程序走不通再尝试仲裁/诉讼与执行

- 协商失败后，依据合同约定的仲裁机构（SIAC、VIAC、HKIAC 或当地仲裁中心）或法院提起程序；
- 裁决/判决作出后，真正难点在**跨境执行**：向债务人所在地法院申请承认与执行，仲裁裁决在《纽约公约》成员国间较易执行，判决则要看双边安排；
- 在执行阶段，配合当地律师调查供应商的账户、存货或不动产，锁定可执行财产。

## 时间与费用预期

| 环节 | 经验周期 | 主要成本 |
|------|---------|---------|
| 证据梳理与发函 | 数天至数周 | 低（函件/翻译费） |
| 协商与调解 | 数周至数月 | 中（律师参与） |
| 仲裁/诉讼至裁决 | 6–12 个月不等，视法域 | 律师费+仲裁/诉讼费 |
| 跨境承认与执行 | 6–18 个月，视法域 | 执行地律师费+法院费用 |

以上均为经验区间，具体取决于货值、争议复杂度、对方所在地法与付款情况——**我们不承诺结果**，但尽早固定证据、及时发函往往决定能否追回损失。

## 风险提示

- 切忌只靠口头交涉不留书面证据，东南亚法院对书面形式要求普遍较高；
- 不要忽视公证、翻译与认证程序，否则证据可能不被采信；
- 不要被"保证追回"话术误导，跨语言跨法域纠纷变量多，请对方书面说明路径与收费；
- 中国法律与越南、印尼、泰国法律差异大，拿不准的以当地法律/专业意见为准。

# Supplier Disputes in Southeast Asia: Practical Guide

You placed an order with a supplier in Vietnam, Indonesia, or Thailand for a better price and steady capacity — then the shipments kept slipping, the goods failed inspection, or the supplier went silent. Southeast Asia offers cost advantages, but disputes across distance, language, and different legal systems can quickly cost more than the goods themselves. And the rules vary sharply by country: civil-law Vietnam, Indonesia's distinctive commercial rules, and Thailand's strict approach to evidence forms. Applying Chinese practice by rote usually fails. Every day of delay gives the supplier time to resell the goods, wind up the company, and make your claim impossible. Here is a practical path built around how each country actually operates.

## Step 1: secure written evidence before any negotiation

- **Archive contracts and communications**: purchase orders, invoices, packing lists, inspection reports, WhatsApp/email threads, and payment records; note delivery dates, quality standards, acceptance methods, and the dispute-resolution clause;
- **Send a written Notice of Breach**: deliver a letter (email plus courier) stating the breach, the cure period, and the amount claimed — often a procedural requirement before local courts will hear you;
- **Confirm the governing law and forum**: many Southeast Asian contracts are silent on these points, so clarify the overall agreement early and whether notarized translation is required.

## Step 2: pick the path that fits each country

- **Vietnam**: commercial arbitration (e.g., VIAC) is common; if the contract names an arbitral forum, use it. Vietnamese civil procedure places heavy weight on written evidence — keep original documents.
- **Indonesia**: disputes generally proceed by court litigation or enforcement of awards, and Indonesian law strictly requires notarization, translation, and legalization of foreign documents — "localize" your evidence before claiming.
- **Thailand**: sensitive to evidence form and limitation periods; send formal written demands, and consider mediation or ADR for smaller amounts at lower cost.
- **Negotiation and settlement**: most supplier disputes still end by agreement — staged re-supply, price concessions, or termination for a partial refund, each committed to a signed written document.

## Step 3: if negotiation fails, turn to arbitration/litigation and enforcement

- After unsuccessful negotiation, start proceedings at the agreed arbitral institution (SIAC, VIAC, HKIAC, or a local center) or a court;
- The real challenge after an award/judgment is **cross-border enforcement**: apply to the courts of the debtor's location for recognition and enforcement — awards travel well among New York Convention members, while judgments depend on bilateral arrangements;
- During enforcement, work with local counsel to locate the supplier's bank accounts, stock, or property to attach.

## Time and cost expectations

| Stage | Typical timeline | Main costs |
|-------|------------------|------------|
| Evidence review and demand letter | Days to weeks | Low (letters/translation) |
| Negotiation and mediation | Weeks to months | Medium (counsel involvement) |
| Arbitration/litigation to award | 6–12 months, by jurisdiction | Counsel + arbitration/court fees |
| Cross-border recognition and enforcement | 6–18 months, by jurisdiction | Enforcement counsel + court costs |

These are experience ranges; actual figures depend on value, complexity, the counterparty's location and law — **we do not promise results**, but securing evidence and issuing a formal notice early often determines whether you recover.

## Key risks

- Do not rely on verbal negotiation without written records — Southeast Asian courts demand written forms;
- Do not overlook notarization, translation, and authentication, or your evidence may be excluded;
- Do not be misled by "guaranteed recovery" promises — cross-language, cross-law disputes have many variables; ask for the path, credentials, and fees in writing;
- China law differs sharply from Vietnamese, Indonesian, and Thai law; when in doubt, follow local law and professional advice.
